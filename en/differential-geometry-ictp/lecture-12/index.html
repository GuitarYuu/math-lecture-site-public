    Lecture 12¶

    Chapter: The Geometry of the Gauss Map

    Developable Ruled Surfaces¶

    Consider a ruled surface

    \[ X(t,v)=\alpha(t)+vw(t). \]

    Example: tangent surface to a curve

    Taking \(w(t)=\alpha'(t)\) gives

    \[ X(t,v)=\alpha(t)+v\alpha'(t). \]

    This is the tangent surface of \(\alpha\), one of the types in the classification below.

    For a general ruled surface,

    \[ X_t=\alpha'+vw', X_v=w, \]
    \[ X_{tt}=\alpha''+vw'', X_{tv}=w', X_{vv}=0. \]

    The following computations apply wherever \(X\) is a regular parametrization. In general, \(X\) may fail to be injective or regular at some points. The unit normal is

    \[ N=\frac{X_t\times X_v}{|X_t\times X_v|}. \]

    The coefficients of the first fundamental form are

    \[ E=|\alpha'+vw'|^2, F=\langle\alpha',w\rangle+v\langle w',w\rangle, G=|w|^2. \]

    Write the scalar triple product as

    \[ (a,b,c)=\langle a,b\times c\rangle. \]

    For the second fundamental form,

    \[ f=\frac{(\alpha',w,w')}{|\alpha'\times w+vw'\times w|}, g=0. \]

    Hence

    \[ K=-\frac{(\alpha',w,w')^2}{|\alpha'\times w+vw'\times w|^2}\frac{1}{EG-F^2}\leq0, \]

    and \(K\equiv0\) if and only if \((\alpha',w,w')=0\). Without loss of generality, take \(|w|=1\); then \(\langle w,w'\rangle=0\).

    Definition

    A ruled surface is called developable if

    \[ (\alpha',w,w')=0. \]

    We now classify developable surfaces.

    1. If \(w\times w'=0\), then \(w'(t)=\lambda(t)w(t)\). Since
    \[ 0=\langle w,w'\rangle=\lambda\langle w,w\rangle=\lambda, \]

    we obtain \(w'\equiv0\) and \(w(t)=w_0\), so the surface is a cylinder.

    1. If \(w\times w'\neq0\), then \(\{\alpha',w,w'\}\) is linearly dependent, so
    \[ \alpha'(t)=\lambda(t)w(t)+\mu(t)w'(t). \]

    Set

    \[ \widetilde{\alpha}(t)=\alpha(t)-\mu(t)w(t). \]

    Then

    \[ \widetilde{\alpha}'=\alpha'-\mu'w-\mu w'=(\lambda-\mu')w. \]

    If \(\widetilde{\alpha}'\equiv0\), then \(\widetilde{\alpha}\equiv\alpha_0\), and therefore

    \[ \alpha(t)=\alpha_0+\mu(t)w(t), \]

    and

    \[ X(t,v)=\alpha_0+(\mu(t)+v)w(t), \]

    so the surface is a cone.

    If \(\widetilde{\alpha}'\neq0\), then the identity above gives

    \[ X(t,v)=\widetilde{\alpha}(t)+(\mu(t)+v)w(t) =\widetilde{\alpha}(t)+\frac{\mu(t)+v}{\lambda(t)-\mu'(t)}\widetilde{\alpha}'(t), \]

    so the surface is the tangent surface of \(\widetilde{\alpha}\).

    Cylinders, cones, and tangent surfaces therefore form a large class of surfaces with identically zero Gauss curvature. This brings us closer to the geometric meaning of Gauss curvature: curvature should distinguish flat surfaces from curved ones.

    The First Fundamental Form and Area¶

    The length of a curve \(\alpha\) is

    \[ L(\alpha)=\int|\alpha'(t)|dt. \]

    Suppose \(\alpha=X\circ\beta\), where \(\beta(t)=(u(t),v(t))\). Then

    \[ |\alpha'(t)|=\left(u'^2E+2u'v'F+v'^2G\right)^{\frac12}, \]

    whereas the Euclidean length of \(\beta\) in the parameter domain is

    \[ L(\beta)=\int\left(u'^2+v'^2\right)^{\frac12}dt. \]

    Thus the first fundamental form measures exactly how \(X\) distorts lengths from the coordinate domain. Moreover,

    \[ |X_u\times X_v|=(EG-F^2)^{\frac12}. \]

    Definition

    If \(V\) lies in the parameter domain, then

    \[ \operatorname{Area}(X(V))=\int_V(EG-F^2)^{\frac12}dudv. \]

    If two charts cover the same region, does its area depend on the chart? This is an exercise.

    The first fundamental form therefore controls two basic geometric quantities on a surface: lengths of curves and areas of regions.

    Minimal Surfaces and Normal Variations¶

    When studying surfaces with zero Gauss curvature, we considered ruled surfaces. Surfaces with zero mean curvature lead instead to minimal surfaces.

    Definition

    A regular surface is called minimal if \(H\equiv0\).

    Let \(X:U\to S\) be a local chart, let \(D\subset U\) be a bounded domain, and let \(h:\overline D\to\mathbb{R}\) be differentiable. The normal variation of \(X(\overline D)\) determined by \(h\) is

    \[ \varphi:\overline D\times(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to\mathbb{R}^3, \]
    \[ \varphi(u,v,t)=X(u,v)+th(u,v)N(u,v). \]

    For \(t\in(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\), write

    \[ X^t(u,v)=\varphi(u,v,t). \]

    Then

    \[ X_u^t=X_u+th_uN+thN_u, X_v^t=X_v+th_vN+thN_v. \]

    Keeping terms through first order in \(t\) gives

    \[ E^t=E-2the+O(t^2), F^t=F-2thf+O(t^2), G^t=G-2thg+O(t^2). \]

    Therefore

    \[ E^tG^t-(F^t)^2 =EG-F^2-2th(Eg-2Ff+Ge)+O(t^2) =(EG-F^2)(1-4thH)+O(t^2). \]

    It follows that

    \[ \operatorname{Area}(X^t(\overline D)) =\int_{\overline D}(EG-F^2)^{\frac12}(1-4thH)^{\frac12}dudv+O(t^2), \]

    and

    \[ \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\operatorname{Area}(X^t(\overline D)) =-\int_{\overline D}2hH(EG-F^2)^{\frac12}dudv. \]

    If \(X\) is minimal, the first variation of area vanishes for every normal variation. Conversely, if

    \[ \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\operatorname{Area}(X^t(\overline D))=0 \]

    for every \(h\), then \(H\equiv0\). This is the variational characterization of minimal surfaces.

    The plane is a minimal surface. The catenoid and helicoid are also minimal surfaces.

    On the word ‘minimal’

    The name “minimal surface” can be misleading: the calculation above shows that the surface is a critical point of the area functional, not necessarily a minimum. Establishing a local minimum would require computing the second variation and proving that it is nonnegative. In this theory, “minimal” therefore means critical before it means minimizing.

    Given a closed curve as boundary, does there exist a minimal surface spanning it? This is the starting point of the Plateau problem.

    Physical model

    If area is interpreted as energy, one is naturally led to soap films. Soap films are the standard physical model for minimal-surface theory.
