    Chapter: The Geometry of the Gauss Map

    \(f:S\to \mathbb{R}\quad p\mapsto|p-p_0|^2\).

    \(p\) critical for \(f\) if \(p_0\in p+\lambda N(p)\) for some \(\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\).

    \((d^2 f)_p(v)=\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\bigg|_{t=0}|\alpha(t)-p_0|^2=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}(2<\alpha'(t),\alpha(t)-p_0>)=2(<\alpha''(0),p-p_0>+|v|^2)=2(|v|^2-\lambda<\alpha''(0),N(p)>)=2(|v|^2-\lambda\mathrm{II}_p(v))\).

    Corollary

    If \(S\) is compact（注：This topological assumption was there only to guarantee the existence of a minimum or a maximum. If for some other reason you know that there is a point for which your surface has a minimum or a maximum, the corollary will also be true.）, then \(\exists p\in S\) s.t. \(K(p)\ge0\).

    Proof

    Pf: \(\exists p_1\) min for \(f\) and \(p_2\) max for \(f\).

    W.L.O.G. Take the maximum, \((d^2f)_p(v)<0\implies|v|^2<\lambda\mathrm{II}_p(v)\).

    This implies what I want if I can argue that \(\lambda\) is positive. I can make \(\lambda\) to be whatever I want, positive or negative. I mean, the value is not free to choose. But I'm free to choose the sign. Because if \(\lambda\) is positive, I'm done. If \(\lambda\) is negative, I reverse \(N\).

    At the minimum point, \((d^2f)_p(v)>0\). I seem to get the wrong inequality, but I can make \(\lambda\) to be the one I want and I make it again the good inequality.\(\square\)

    Hilbert's theorem: \(S\) oriented, \(k_1\le k_2\) the principal curvatures. Suppose there exists \(p\in S\) s.t. 1) \(K(p)>0\). 2) \(k_1\) has a local min at \(p\). 3) \(k_2\) has a local max at \(p\). Then \(p\) is umbilical.

    Pf: Our geometry is kind f \(O_3\) plus translation invariant in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), so just for convenience, we can assume that ou special point.

    We can assume \(p=(0,0,0)\) and \(N(p)=(0,0,1)\).

    \(e_1=(1,0,0),e_2=(0,1,0)\) are the principal directions at \(p\).

    Around \(p\), \(S\) is locally the graph of a differentiable function \(f\).

    \(X(u,v)=(u,v,f(u,v)),f(0,0)=0,f_u(0,0)=0,f_v(0,0)=0\).

    \(X_u(0,0)=e_1,X_v(0,0)=e_2\).

    \(e=\frac{f_{uu}}{\sqrt{1+f_u^2+f_v^2}},f=\frac{f_{uv}}{\sqrt{1+f_u^2+f_v^2}},g=\frac{f_{vv}}{\sqrt{1+f_u^2+f_v^2}}\).（注：These are true on the whole neighborhood. These are general formula for the coefficients of the second fundamental form of the graph, everywhere covered by the graph chart.）

    \(f_{uu}(0,0)=k_1(p),f_{uv}(0,0)=0,f_{vv}(0,0)=k_2(p).\)

    define: \(\alpha(u)=X(u,0),\beta(v)=X(0,v)\).

    \(E_1(v)=\frac{1}{|X_u(0,v)|}X_u(0,v)\in T_{\beta(v)}S\).

    \(E_2(v)=\frac{1}{|X_v(u,0)|}X_v(u,0)\in T_{\alpha(u)}S\).

    \(h_1(v)=\mathrm{II}_{\beta(v)}(E_1(v),E_1(v)),h_2(u)=\mathrm{II}_{\alpha(u)}(E_2(u))\)

    \(h_1(v)=\frac{f_{uu}}{\sqrt{1+f_u^2+f_v^2}(1+f_u^2)}(0,v),h_2(u)=\frac{f_{vv}}{\sqrt{1+f_u^2+f_v^2}(1+f_v^2)}(u,0)\).

    \(h_2(0)=\mathrm{II}_p(e_2)=k_2(p)\ge k_2(\alpha(u))\ge\mathrm{II}_{\alpha(u)}(E_2(u))=h_2(u)\).

    so 0 is a local max for \(h_2\).

    \(h_1(0)=\mathrm{II}_p(e_1)=k_1(p)\le k_1(\beta(v))\le\mathrm{II}_{\beta(v)}(E_1(v))=h_1(v)\).

    so 0 is a local min for \(h_1\implies h_2''(0)\le0\le h_1''(0)\).

    We got the expressions here and we want to express it in terms of \(f\).

    \(h_2'(0)=[-(1+f_v^2)^{-2}2f_vf_{vu}(1+f_u^2+f_v^2)^{-\frac{1}{2}}f_{vv}-\frac{1}{2}(1+f_u^2+f_v^2)^{-\frac{3}{2}}(1+f_v^2)^{-1}f_{vv}(2f_uf_{uu}+2f_vf_{uv})+(1+f_u^2+f_v^2)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+f_v^2)^{-1}f_{vvu}](u,0)\).

    Recall \(f_{uu}(0,0)=k_1,f_{u,v}(0,0)=0,f_{vv}=k_2,f_u(0,0)=0,f_v(0,0)=0,f(0,0)=0\).

    \(h_2''(0)=-f_{vv}(0)f_{uu}f_{uu}(0)+f_{vvuu}(0)\).

    \(h_1''(0)=-f_{vv}f_{vv}f_{uu}(0)+f_{vvuu}(0)\).

    \(h_2''(0)-h_1''(0)=f_{uu}f_{vv}(0)(f_{vv}(0)-f_{uu}(0))=K(p)(k_2(p)-k_1(p))\le0\).

    Now, how is it possible that the product of two numbers is non-positive? Well, if the first one is positive, the second one must be negative. But how is it possible? This is big minus small. So the only possibility is actually they are the same. So this implies the theorem.\(\square\)

    Corollary

    \(S\) compact and connected and \(K>0\) everywhere. Suppose \(H\) is constant. Then \(S\) is a sphere.（注：In this case, I don't have to be worried about it's a piece of the sphere because it's compact. So it has to be the whole sphere.）

    Proof

    Pf: Let \(c\in\mathbb{R}\) s.t. \(H(p)\equiv c,\forall p\in S\).

    \(c\not\equiv0\) because otherwise \(k_1=-k_2\implies K\le 0\).

    Claim: \(S\) is orientable.

    Suppose at a given point I choose \(N\) in such a way that \(H\) with this choice of \(N\) is equal to \(c\). I said \(H\) is equal to \(c\), but \(H\) depends on the choice of \(N\). Because otherwise it becomes \(-c\). So if you give me one choice, it's \(c\). If you give me the opposite choice, the only other possibility is \(-N\), I got \(-c\). This forces the normal vector to be one of the two possibilities at every point. There is only one if the two for which \(H\) is equal to \(c\). See, once we observe that \(c\) is non-zero, this determines a choice of \(N\). But this \(N\) has to be globally defined. Because if it stops to be globally defined at some point, it means at some point it has become \(-N\), but then \(H\) would have become \(-c\). The choice of the value of the mean curvature determines a unique normal vector for which that is the value.

    Why is it important? If I have an orientable thing, I have a choice of \(N\) everywhere, the Gauss curvature, the principal curvature, the mean curvature, everywhere become globally defined. So this is the advantage of the important thing.

    \(\implies k_1,k_2:S\to \mathbb{R}\) are well defined and continuous.

    Take \(p\) s.t. \(k_1\) has a local min at \(p\).

    \(p\) is a local max for \(k_2\), because \(H\equiv c\).

    \(\implies p\) is umbilical.

    Actually, if I want to prove that something is a sphere, the only gun I have is to prove that every point is umbilical.

    Take \(q\in S,k_2(q)\le k_2(p)=k_1(p)\le k_1(q)\implies k_2(q)=k_1(q)\).

    So our surface is covered by umbilical points, every point is umbilical.\(\square\)

    Now you can see, this proof is actually very flexible and the most famous. Actually, this was the origin of Hilbert's theorem. But actually, Hilbert himself proved another corollary of his argument, which in some sense, it's even more interesting, and this has passed through history as Hilbert-Liebmann theorem.

    Corollary

    \(S\) compact and connected. If \(K\) is a constant, then \(S\) is a sphere.

    Proof

    Pf: \(K\) must be positive.（注：Every compact surface has a point whose Gauss curvature is greater than or equal to zero.）

    \(\implies H\) is non-zero everywhere.

    \(\implies S\) orientable.

    \(k_1,k_2:S\to \mathbb{R}\).

    \(p\) min for \(k_1\implies p\) max for \(k_2\implies p\) umbilical \(\implies\) every point is umbilical.\(\square\)

    Now, of course, what does a geometer in this sense when studies this subject, you have defined many functions, \(k_1,k_2,H,K\). You would like to classify surfaces by looking at these curvatures. Which are the surfaces for which one or two or three? It's the first question in a general classification theorem.
