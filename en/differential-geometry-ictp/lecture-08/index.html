    Chapter: The Geometry of the Gauss Map

    Example

    3) Cylinder: \(S=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^3\mid x^2+y^2=r^2\},r>0\).

    \(f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2,S=f^{-1}(r^2)\).

    \(df_p=2(x,y,0),\nabla f_p=2(x,y,0),N(p)=\frac{\nabla f_p}{|\nabla f_p|}=\frac{1}{r}(x,y,0).\)

    \(T_pS=ker\ df_p=\{v\mid <\nabla f_p,v>=0\},v=(v_1,v_2,v_3)\).

    \(dN_p(v)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}(N\circ\alpha)(t)=\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\frac{1}{r}(x(t),y(t),0)=\frac{1}{r}(v_1,v_2,0)\).

    \(\mathrm{II}_p(v,w)=-\frac{1}{r}(v_1w_1+v_2w_2)\).

    \(v\) vertical（注：vertical means it has only z component）, \(dN_p(v)=0\).

    \(v\) tangent to the circle, \(dN_p(v)=\frac{1}{r}v\).

    \(k_1(p)=-\frac{1}{r},k_2(p)=0\).

    \(K(p)=0,H(p)=-\frac{1}{2r}\).

    If it's a plane, \(K\) is zero, but it's not the unique surface having zero curvature. What's going on? I don't want to give you the answer now, but focus on this problem.

    Geometric Meaning of \(\mathrm{II}_p\):

    Definition

    \(\alpha:I\to S\) regular curve, \(\alpha(0)=p\).

    \(k\) curvature of \(\alpha\) as a curve in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).（注：If I study the surface, I have a normal vector to the curve, and we called it capital \(N\), unfortunately. So now we have to distinguish the two. Because there is absolutely no reason why the normal to the surface and the normal to the curve should have anything to do at all.）

    \(\cos\theta=<n,N>\), \(n\) normal to \(\alpha\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

    \(k_n=k\cos \theta\) is called the normal curvature of \(\alpha\) at \(p\).

    Of course, \(k_n\) depends not just on \(\alpha\), but on \(S\). So in some sense, it's the projection of the curvature to the surface.

    Now suppose that \(\alpha\) is p.a.l. \(N(s)=N(\alpha(s))\).

    \(<N(s),\alpha'(s)>\equiv0\implies <N',\alpha'>+<N,\alpha''>=0\).

    \(\mathrm{II}_p(\alpha'(0),\alpha'(0))=-<dN_p(\alpha'(0)),\alpha'(0)>=-<N'(0),\alpha'(0)>=<N(p),\alpha''(0)>=<N(p),k(p)n(p)>=k_n(p)\).

    So if \(v\in T_pS,|v|=1,\mathrm{II}_p(v)\) is equal to the normal curvature of any curve passing through \(p\) with velocity \(v\).

    Theorem

    Any curve passing through the point with a given velocity has the same normal curvature.

    The eigenvalues of the quadratic form are the critical points of the value of the quadratic form restricted to the sphere in the vector space.

    \(k_1(p),k_2(p)\) are the minimum and maximum of \(\mathrm{II}_p\) restricted to the vectors of \(|\cdot|=1\).

    If I take any curve passing through the point on the surface, its normal curvature is bounded between \(k_1\) and \(k_2\).

    \(e_1,e_2\) O.N. basis of \(T_pS\) of eigenvectors of \(-dN_p\), \(v=\cos\theta\ e_1+\sin\theta\ e_2\).

    Narrative

    (Euler's Formula) \(k_n(v)=\mathrm{II}_p(v)=-<dN_p(v),v>=-<dN_p(\cos\theta\ e_1+\sin\theta\ e_2),\cos\theta\ e_1+\sin\theta\ e_2>=-<\cos\theta\ k_1e_1+\sin\theta\ k_2e_2,\cos\theta\ e_1+\sin\theta\ e_2>=-\cos^2\theta\ k_1-\sin^2\theta\ k_2\).

    Definition

    \(p\in S\) is called

    1) elliptic if \(K(p)>0\).

    2) hyperbolic if \(K(p)<0\).

    3) parabolic if \(K(p)=0,dN_p\neq0\).（注：But we have two ways of getting zero curvature. And here we want to make a difference the plane and the cylinder. The Gauss curvature is the product of th two numbers. It can be zero in two ways. They are both zero or one is zero and the other is not. See, and this is exactly the difference between plane and cylinder. Parabolic is somehow the cylinder in your mind.）

    4) planar if \(K(p)=0,dN_p\equiv0\).

    5) if \(k_1(p)=k_2(p)\), \(p\) is called umbilical.

    Do planar points characterize the plane? I mean, the number is no. There are surfaces which have planar points, and the surfaces are not the plane.

    Exercise

    \(S_{p_0}^2(r)\) is made of umbilical points.

    Note that if a surface has an umbilical point, then it is a plane or a sphere. That would be really too much. One point cannot tell you the global geometry of a surface.

    Proposition

    If \(S\) is connected（注：because otherwise you have to argue on each connected component. In principle, your surface would be a plane and a sphere. As long as they don't touch, a plane union a sphere, it's a regular surface） and every \(p\in S\) is umbilical, then \(S\) is contained in a plane or in a sphere.（注：I cannot say \(S\) is a plane or a sphere because maybe it's just a piece of.）

    Proof

    Pf: \(X:U\to S\) local chart. \(w\in T_pS,w=aX_u+bX_v,dN_p(w)=\lambda(p)w\).

    In fact, I want now, in some sense, to prove that \(\lambda\) does not depend on \(p\). That's the line of the proof.

    \(aN_u+bN_v=\lambda(p)(aX_u+bX_v),\forall a,b\).

    \(\implies\)（注：\(\lambda\) in principle was nothing. Had no regularity whatever. You can argue automatically that it should be a continuous function. Because it's the eigenvalue of a differentiable family of maps. But eigenvalues of a differentiable family of maps are just continuous. They're not more than continuous.） $N_u=\lambda X_u,N_u=\lambda X_v\implies \lambda $ is \(C^{\infty}\).

    \(\lambda_vX_u+\lambda X_{uv}=\lambda_uX_v+\lambda X_{uv}\).

    \(\lambda_vX_u=\lambda_uX_v\implies \lambda_u=\lambda_v=0\).

    1) If \(\lambda=0\implies N\) is constant \(\implies\subset\) plane.

    2) \(\lambda\neq0\implies X(u,v)-\frac{1}{\lambda}N(u,v)=c\) constant vector \(\implies |X(u,v)-c|^2=\frac{1}{|\lambda|^2}\).

    Just one thing, how come in our computation, \(\lambda\), which is the \(k_1\) or \(k_2\)? In our computation, it was plus or minus one over \(r\). Why you should not be surprised? So you could have said, well, if this \(\lambda\) is negative, it's a sphere. But no, this is dearly a wrong theorem. Because if I switch \(N\), you see, in this proposition, there is no \(N\). So \(\lambda\) on the same surface can be positive or negative because I pick one choice of \(N\), I get positive, for example, I mean, in the way we compute it, we got negative. If we do the other choice, we got positive. So you see, there is no case two \(\lambda\) positive, case three \(\lambda\) negative. It would be completely wrong. So zero and no zero, these are the only two geometric distinctions. But positive or negative here, it would be crazy.

    What do we prove up to now? We have proved that the image of a chart is contained either in a plane or in the sphere. But then the whole, since every point is contained in a chart. So there is only one thing to be worried about. Would it be possible, on a surface, to have two charts: one contains on a sphere of some center and some radius and the other one contained either in another sphere of another center and another radius, or, even worse, on a plane. So I mean, why these two cases? These two cases were built with the local chart, so if you change chart, in principle, you could jump from one to two. And in fact, two, you could jump from different centers and different radius. But no, by continuity. This function \(\lambda\), is defined over the whole surface and it's continuous over the whole surface. So I mean, \(\lambda\) cannot be one here and zero here. In principle it could, but since I could join on the surface, I can go from any one point to the other with a sequence of charts intersecting each other. Of course, by connectness. But then, on the overlap, \(\lambda\) has to be constant. So it has to be the same on the two sides. So certainly I cannot pass from a sphere to a sphere, the two spheres must have the same radius. So now is it possible from one sphere to the other sphere of the same radius, I change center? Exercise. But this in principle, is possible from the way we have built things, because \(c\), which is the center of the sphere, is just kind of a constant of integration.\(\square\)

    Proposition

    \(K\) and \(H\) are smooth functions. \(k_1,k_2\) are continuous functions, smooth on the open set of non-umbilical points.

    Proof

    Pf: \(X:U\to S\) local chart, \(N=\frac{X_u\wedge X_v}{|X_u\wedge X_v|},X_u,X_v.\)

    \(\mathrm{I}_p\to M\)（注：the first fundamental form is represented by the matrix \(M\)） \(\qquad\mathrm{II}_p\to \sum\qquad\) \(-dN_p\to A\).

    \(M= \begin{pmatrix} |X_u|^2 & <X_u,X_v>\\ <X_u,x_v> & |X_v|^2\\ \end{pmatrix},E=|X_u|^2,F=<X_u,X_v>,G=|X_v|^2\).

    \(\sum= \begin{pmatrix} e & f\\ f & g\\ \end{pmatrix},e=\mathrm{II}_p(X_u,X_u)=<N,X_{uu}>=-<N_u,X_u>,f=\mathrm{II}_p(X_u,X_v)=<N,X_{uv}>,e=\mathrm{II}_p(X_v,X_v)=<N,X_{vv}>.\)

    \((a_{ij})=A=-(+?)M^{-1}\sum=-\begin{pmatrix} E & F\\ F & G\\ \end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} e & f\\ f & g\\ \end{pmatrix}=-\frac{1}{EG-F^2}\begin{pmatrix} G & -F\\ -F & E\\ \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} e & f\\ f & g\\ \end{pmatrix}\).

    \(a_{11}=\frac{Ff-Ge}{EG-F^2},a_{12}=\frac{Fg-Gf}{EG-F^2},a_{21}=\frac{Fe-Ef}{EG-F^2},a_{22}=\frac{Ff-Eg}{EG-F^2}\).

    \(K=det\ A=\frac{eF-fE}{EG-F^2}\).（注：Just one moment, everybody is doing a little bit od geometry of surfaces should remember by heart this formula.）

    \(H=-\frac{tr(A)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{eG+gE-2fF}{EG-F^2}\).

    \(k_i=H\pm\sqrt{H^2-K}\).

    The square root, of course, is smooth, as long as what's inside is different from zero. The average is equal to the determinant if and only if the two eigenvalues are the same, so you are at an umbilical point. So outside the umbilical point, \(k_i\) is smooth.\(\square\)
