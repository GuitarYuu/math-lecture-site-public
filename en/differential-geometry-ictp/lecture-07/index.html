    Lecture 7¶

    Chapter: The Geometry of the Gauss Map

    Definition

    \(S\) is a regular surface. A vector field \(V\) on \(S\) is a differentiable function \(V:S\to\mathbb{R}^3\). \(V\) is called normal (unitary) if \(\forall p\in S,V(p)\perp T_pS\ (|V(p)|=1)\).

    Example

    \(S=\{2z=x^2+y^2\}\).

    \(S=\Gamma_f,f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}\quad (x,y)\mapsto\frac{1}{2}(x^2+y^2)\).

    \(X:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^3\quad (u,v)\mapsto(u,v,\frac{1}{2}(u^2+v^2))\).

    \(\{\frac{\partial X}{\partial u},\frac{\partial X}{\partial v}\},X_u=(1,0,u),X_v=(0,1,v)\).

    \(p=X_(u_0,v_0),T_pS=span\{X_u(u_0,v_0),X_v(u_0,v_0)\}\).

    Now, the problem is, how many normal vectors do we have at a given point? Two.

    Narrative

    Now, the point is, the normal vectors were at a fixed point. Now let's start moving the point and let's see what happens. I mean, how many choices we have globally on a surface of unitary normal vectors. So let's see first what happens not globally on the surface, but on the piece of the surface which is covered by a chart.

    Lemma

    \(X;U\to S\) is a local chart. Then there exists at least one unitary normal vector field on \(X(U)\).

    Proof

    Pf:notation:\(\frac{\partial X}{\partial u}=X_u,\frac{\partial X}{\partial v}=X_v\).

    \(\{X_u,X_v\}\) is a basis of \(T_{X(q)}S,q\in U\).

    Put \(N^X(q)=\frac{X_u(q)\wedge X_v(q)}{|X_u(q)\wedge X_v(q)|}\).

    \(N^X:U\to\mathbb{R}^3\) is differentiable. \(N^X(q)\perp T_{X(q)}S,|N^X(q)|=1\).

    Define \(N(p)=N^X\circ X^{-1}(p)\).\(\square\)

    Lemma

    \(S\) is connected. \(N_1,N_2\) are unitary normal vector fields on \(S\). Then either \(N_1=N_2\) or \(N_1=-N_2\).

    Proof

    Pf:\(\forall p\in S, N_1(p),N_2(p)\) are unitary normals at \(p\), so \(N_1(p)=N_2(p)\) or \(N_1(p)=-N_2(p)\).

    \(S=A\cup B,A=\{p\in S\mid N_1(p)=N_2(p)\},B=\{q\in S\mid N_1(q)=-N_2(q)\}\).

    \(A,B\) closed \(\implies\) \(A\) or \(B\) empty.\(\square\)

    So on a whole connected component, I have only two choices of unitary normal. But now the question is, does there always exist a unitary normal on a surface? Remember, locally, yes, because every surface is \(X\) of \(U\). So on the piece of the surface which is given like \(X\) of \(U\) for some chart, there is a unitary normal. But who is going to tell me if there is a unitary normal over the whole surface? In fact, it may be false. So the surface where a unitary normal exists are special.

    Definition

    A regular surface \(S\), s.t., \(\exists\) unitary normal \(N:S\to\mathbb{R}^3\), is called orientable.

    Example

    1) \(S_{p_0}^2(r)\).

    \(N:S_{p_0}^2(r)\to\mathbb{R}^3\quad p\mapsto\frac{1}{r}(p-p_0)\).

    2) \(S=\Gamma_f\quad X(u,v)=(u,v,f(u,v))\).

    \(N^X=\frac{(1,0,f_u)\wedge (0,1,f_v)}{|(1,0,f_u)\wedge (0,1,f_v)|}=\frac{(f_u,f_v,-1)}{\sqrt{1+f_u^2+f_v^2}}\).

    3) \(S=f^{-1}(a),p \in S\). \(T_pS=ker\ df_p=\{v\in \mathbb{R}^3\mid<\nabla f(p),v>=0\}\).

    \(N=\frac{\nabla f}{|\nabla f|}\).

    The key problem of this course is, what does it mean that something is flat? And what does it mean that something has some curvature.

    \(N:S\to\mathbb{R}^3\) unitary normal, \(|N(p)|=1\).

    \(N:S\to S_0^2(1)\) is called Gauss map.

    \(dN_p:T_pS\to T_{N(p)}S_0^2(1)\simeq\{N(p)\}^{\perp}\simeq T_pS\).

    \(dN_p:T_pS\to T_pS\).

    Theorem

    \(dN_p\) is a symmetric（注：symmetric means \(<dN_p(v),w>=<v,dN_p(w)>,\forall v,w\in T_pS\)） endomorphism（注：endomorphism means just it's a nomomorphism of vectors of the vector space to itself） of \(T_pS\).

    Proof

    Pf: It is enough to check it on basis. \(p\in S,X:U\to S\) chart around \(p\). \(\{X_u,X_v\}\) basis of \(T_pS\).

    \(\alpha(t)=X(u(t),v(t)),\alpha(0)=p\).

    \(dN_p(\alpha'(0))=dN_p(u'(0)X_u+v'(0)X_v)=u'(0)dN_p(X_u)+v'(0)dN_p(X_v)=u'(0)N_u+v'(0)N_v\).

    \(\frac{\partial}{\partial v}<N,X_u>=0=<N_v,X_u>+<N,X_{uv}>\).

    \(\frac{\partial}{\partial u}<N,X_v>=0=<N_u,X_v>+<N,X_{vu}>\).

    \(<N_u,X_v>=<X_u,N_v>=-<N,X_{uv}>\).\(\square\)

    Definition

    1) \(\mathrm{II}_p:T_pS\times T_pS\to\mathbb{R}\quad (v,w)\mapsto -<dN_p(v),w>\) is called the second fundamental form of \(S\) at \(p\).

    2) \(\mathrm{I}_p: T_pS\times T_pS\to\mathbb{R}\quad (v,w)\mapsto <v,w>\) is called the first fundamental form \(S\) at \(p\).

    3) \(N_p\) symmetric \(\implies\) diagonalizable.

    \(k_1(p),k_2(p)\) the eigenvalues of \(-dN_p\ (k_1(p)\le k_2(p))\) are called principal curvature of \(S\) at \(p\).

    4) \(K(p)=k_1(p)\cdot k_2(p)\) is called Gauss(ian) curvature of \(S\) at \(p\).

    5) \(H(p)=\frac{1}{2}(k_1(p)+k_2(p))\) is called the Mean curvature of \(S\) at \(p\).

    6) the directions associated to the eigenvectors

    \(v_1,v_2\) associated to \(k_1,k_2\) are called principal directions.

    7) \(\alpha:I\to S\) is called a line of curvature if \(\alpha'(t)\) is a principal direction of \(S\) at \(\alpha(t),\forall t\).

    Example

    0) \(S=\)plane \(N:S\to S^2\).

    \(dN_p(v)=0,\forall v\).

    principal curvature of the plane, both zero.

    Gauss curvature: zero.

    Mean curvature: zero.

    principal directions: There are infinitely many in this case, every direction is a principal direction.

    lines of curvature: any curve on a plane.

    1) \(S=S_{p_0}^2(r)\).

    \(N:S_{p_0}^2(r)\to S^2\quad p\mapsto\frac{1}{r}(p-p_0)\).

    \(dN_p(v)=\frac{1}{r}v\).

    \(\mathrm{II}_p(v,w)=-\frac{1}{r}<v,w>\).

    \(H(p)=k_1(p)=k_2(p)=-\frac{1}{r},\forall p\).

    \(K(p)=\frac{1}{r^2}\).

    Any tangent direction is a principal direction.

    Any curve is a line of curvature.

    Choice of the orientation has the effect of switching sign to \(H,k_1\) and \(k_2\) and it's irrelevant for the Gauss curvature.
